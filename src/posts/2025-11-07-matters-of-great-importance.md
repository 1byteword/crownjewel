# Things worth working on

AI alignment, world models, brain-computer interfaces, energy, and robotics. These are the five problems I keep coming back to when I think about what to spend my time on.

Most of my day-to-day work is incremental: better tooling, more reliable infrastructure, a system that’s easier to understand. I care about that work. But I also want to keep some attention on the problems that could change what becomes possible in the first place.

Kuhn’s distinction between normal science and scientific revolutions is useful here. A field can make steady progress for years, then change its assumptions and open up an entirely different set of questions. I’m interested in where that might happen next.

This is a working list, with a fairly strong bias toward problems that affect one another.

## AI alignment

If we build systems that can outthink us across enough important domains, I want us to retain some say in what they do. That seems like a prerequisite for being optimistic about much of the rest of this list.

A capable system that shares our aims could help with the reactors, simulations, implants, and robots. A capable system pursuing incompatible aims could make those projects irrelevant to us. That asymmetry is why I put alignment first.

## Better world models

I’d like to be able to try more things in a simulation before trying them in the world.

That is useful almost everywhere: testing a robot’s behavior, looking for a material, designing a reactor. The hard part is knowing whether the simulation gets the relevant details right. A beautiful result inside a model is worth very little if it disappears when you build the thing.

"Perfect world models" is the ambitious version. The practical question is which missing details are holding up a particular experiment, and whether a better model could remove that bottleneck.

## Brain-computer interfaces that last

The part that interests me most is the interface with living tissue. An impressive demonstration is one thing. A device that remains useful for years is a much harder proposition.

I want to understand what it would take to build peripherals that can coexist with the brain, repair themselves, and keep a stable connection. Restoring lost functions is already a reason to care. Longer term, the possibility of a better connection between people and machines raises a much wider set of questions.

## Abundant energy

Cheap solar makes me optimistic. It also makes the remaining problems easier to see: connecting generation to the grid, storing energy, and supplying power when and where sunlight isn’t enough.

I don’t think enthusiasm for solar requires giving up on nuclear. I’m interested in firm power, dense industrial sites, and high-temperature heat as well as the price of a kilowatt-hour. Different jobs place different demands on the equipment.

The transition also extends well beyond electricity. Aviation, shipping, steel, cement, and chemical feedstocks are difficult cases. A lot of the proposed substitutes depend on having much more cheap energy available. That makes energy a recurring constraint on other ambitions, rather than an isolated item on the list.

I want to understand where the costs actually sit: in the physics, in manufacturing, in permitting, or in infrastructure we haven’t built yet. Those call for very different kinds of work.

## General-purpose robotics

A robot has to deal with the world it gets. Objects move, surfaces vary, people behave unexpectedly. A carefully staged demonstration leaves a lot of that difficulty out.

This is where better world models and abundant energy start to meet. Robots need useful models of consequences, and building and running them takes physical resources. I’m interested in how we get from a convincing prototype to machines that can do useful work in ordinary, unprepared places.

## How they fit together

The connections are a large part of the appeal. Energy supports the compute used to train models. Better models could help design machines and materials. Robotics could turn more of those designs into physical things. Brain-computer interfaces draw on progress in materials, fabrication, and control, while opening a different route into the relationship between people and machines.

I don’t know which part I will end up contributing to. For now, I want to follow the dependencies, understand the bottlenecks, and get more specific about what a useful next step would look like.
